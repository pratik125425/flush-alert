After a drought, the standard spring nitrate estimate is off by 25%, 1.8 times its usual error.

On June 12, 2025, Central Iowa Water Works banned lawn watering across the Des Moines metro. Nitrate in the Raccoon and Des Moines rivers was near record. Both springs, 2024 and 2025, followed a year of river flow in the driest quarter of the Raccoon's record.

On springs it had never seen, Flush Alert flagged 72% of post-drought nitrate surges, a median of 17 days before they began, from river flow alone.

Drought leaves nitrate in the soil, and the first wet spring flushes it into rivers within a few weeks, a window twelve samples a year rarely catch. Flush Alert watches river flow alone and tells sampling programs, weeks ahead, which rivers need extra samples and when.

Historical backtest on 33 USGS nitrate-sensor sites and 269 springs, 2009–2026. The same rule runs on 1,241 stream gauges with no sensor, and today puts 330 of them on a spring 2027 watchlist.

It happens everywhere we can check, and drought makes it worse

For every sensor site and spring, we simulated 28 monthly schedules (one per day of the month) and estimated the March–June nitrate load two ways. Regression is the LOADEST-style model agencies use: it predicts concentration from flow and season. Interpolation draws straight lines between samples.

How Flush Alert works

One rule, two inputs: the river's memory of last year, and the first rise of this spring. Both come from the daily discharge every USGS stream gauge already reports.

  1. Drought memory

    On March 1, rank the last 12 months of flow against every earlier day in the gauge's own record. Below the 25th percentile, the spring is Watch.

  2. First wet-spring rise

    In a Watch spring, look for the 7-day mean flow to climb above that calendar day's median from past years, or for flow to jump 25% in three days.

  3. Alert

    The first day either happens, the gauge goes to Alert. Each decision uses only data up to that day. A test in the repository fails if any future value can change it.

  4. Extra samples

    Over the following weeks, take a grab sample on each new flow rise, when the flush arrives, rather than waiting for the next calendar date.

The backtest

We tuned the one free setting, the size of the three-day rise, on springs up to 2019, then froze the rule and tested it on 2020–2026, which includes the 2023–2025 post-drought springs. We also held out each site in turn.

It warns weeks before the surge

72% of held-out post-drought surges were flagged in advance, with a median 17 days before nitrate crossed the surge line and 81 days before the spring peak.

95% range 62%–82% (bootstrap over site-springs). With every site held out in turn, it still caught 66%. A surge is the 7-day mean nitrate passing the site's 90th percentile or 10 mg/L, whichever is lower.

Both halves of the rule earn their place

Removing either input makes the alert worse on the held-out springs. False alarms cost a utility a few extra grab samples. A miss leaves a surge unmeasured, so the rule leans toward warning.

Held-out test springs, 2020–2026

Without drought memory every spring is eligible, so that rule faces 130 surges instead of 75. Critical success index = caught / (caught + missed + false alarms), which accounts for the difference. Held-out springs 2020–2026. results/backtest_metrics.csv

Timing beats volume

Add six grab samples to a post-drought spring at random dates and the regression estimate gets no better. Add the same six when flow says the flush is coming and its error falls from 23.9% (monthly samples alone, on springs with a flow rise to time against) to 18.1%, removing about a quarter of it. With interpolation, extra samples help at almost any timing, and evenly spaced ones help most.

The result holds when the drought line moves

The 25th-percentile cutoff is a choice. Moving it to the 20th or 30th changes how many springs count as post-drought, not the answer.

    Limits and methods

    What this does not show

    • Results come from a historical backtest on sensor data, not a live deployment.
    • The surge line and the drought cutoff are choices. Both are in config.yaml, and the cutoff sensitivity is above.
    • Only sites with sensors can be validated. Flow-only gauges are an extrapolation of the same rule.
    • Post-drought springs are rare, a few per site, so we report bootstrap ranges rather than single numbers.
    • Flush Alert complements the University of Iowa's nitrate forecasts at its Iowa sensors. It schedules sampling, and it runs at gauges with no sensor, in any state.

    Method in brief

    • True load: concentration × discharge × 2.4466, in kg per day.
    • Monthly schedules: one sample per month on each day 1–28, giving 28 histories per site.
    • Regression: ln C = b0 + b1 ln Q + b2 sin 2πt + b3 cos 2πt + b4 t, calibrated on monthly samples within ±1 year, with Duan smearing.
    • No lookahead: the trigger on day t sees data through day t only, enforced by tests in tests/test_no_lookahead.py.
    • Every number on this page is read from a CSV in results/.
